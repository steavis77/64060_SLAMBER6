Introduction

Transform One Variable

For the transformation step, I converted body mass from grams to kilograms. This creates a new variable called body_mass_kg.

penguins$body_mass_kg <- penguins$body_mass_g / 1000

head(penguins[, c("species", "body_mass_g", "body_mass_kg")])
##   species body_mass_g body_mass_kg
## 1  Adelie        3750         3.75
## 2  Adelie        3800         3.80
## 3  Adelie        3250         3.25
## 4  Adelie          NA           NA
## 5  Adelie        3450         3.45
## 6  Adelie        3650         3.65

This transformation makes body mass easier to interpret because kilograms are a more familiar unit than grams for larger body weight values.

Plot One Quantitative Variable

hist(na.omit(penguins$body_mass_kg),
     main = "Distribution of Penguin Body Mass",
     xlab = "Body Mass (kg)",
     ylab = "Count")

The histogram above shows the distribution of penguin body mass in kilograms. This helps visualize the spread of body mass values across the observations in the dataset.

Create a Scatterplot

species_factor <- as.factor(penguins$species)
species_colors <- as.numeric(species_factor)

plot(penguins$flipper_length_mm,
     penguins$body_mass_kg,
     main = "Penguin Body Mass by Flipper Length",
     xlab = "Flipper Length (mm)",
     ylab = "Body Mass (kg)",
     col = species_colors,
     pch = 19)

legend("topleft",
       legend = levels(species_factor),
       col = 1:length(levels(species_factor)),
       pch = 19)

The scatterplot shows the relationship between flipper length and body mass. In general, penguins with longer flippers appear to have higher body mass. Coloring the points by species also makes it easier to compare the different penguin species.
